A Free Thanksgiving Turkey

Thanksgiving was coming up and a supermarket cashier told me that I’d accumulated enough points to redeem a free turkey.

I asked him, “Raw or cooked?”

He rolled his eyes and said, “Raw.”

My mood at that moment: @#%€!

I went home and asked my mom, “Do you know how to make a Thanksgiving turkey dinner?”

Mom honestly admitted, “Nope.”

My mood at that moment: @#%€!×2.

I decided to download an online recipe and be a good, self-reliant person. After reading the recipe, my mood was: @#%€!×3.

So, in sum, my average mood in the lead-up to Thanksgiving is:

@#%€! + @#%€!

That frozen turkey took up most of the space in our freezer for over four weeks. Even after it was finally gone, I couldn’t look at a turkey for a long time without feeling as though it were laughing at me. There’s a Chinese saying: “Wear a hat that fits your head.” I had never imagined that I would one day learn this lesson from a frozen turkey.

Christmas Tree

Last night, my husband and I put up the Christmas tree at home. I sat on the floor, gazing up at it in silence for a long, long time.

I remember our first Christmas after I’d moved to America. My husband and I picked out a tall, plump pine from a nearby Christmas tree lot, tied it to the roof of the car, and drove home~~ ~~humming cheerful little tunes all the way. Once there, we secured it in its red-and -green iron stand, watered it, decorated it, and admired it. That was my first ever Christmas tree.

After the holiday, everyone else in the neighborhood put their Christmas trees out for disposal, but I just couldn't let ours go.

“It’s still drinking water. It’s still alive,” I said to my husband.

We took off the ornaments one by one. Every day, I watered the tree and looked after it, and the tree responded with green.

In March, my husband insisted that we send it away for recycling. I knew I couldn't avoid it forever, so I agreed. But the next day, tiny little growths appeared all over the tree, shaped like pinecones but incredibly small, as though the tree had burst into bloom with little brown flowers.

“It’s telling us it’s still alive,” I told him.

And so, it stayed.

I watered it and spoke to it every day.

By May, it was no longer drinking water. Each day, countless pine needles fell beneath it, yet the tree remained green enough and its little blossoms still bloomed.

When my husband finally dragged the tree from the living room into the backyard, pine needles scattered all along the way, like tears left behind. And my heart fell right along with them.

From that year on, there would be no more real Christmas trees under our roof. We bought a plastic tree from a store instead, putting it up and decorating it every Christmas, then taking it down and storing it away when the season was over.

No partings. No regrets.

But in my mind, I can never quite forget that very first Christmas tree—the real pine whose living green kept me company for nearly five months.

Perhaps it is the inevitable goodbye that makes the time spent together all the more precious.

The Blimp

I pointed to the blimp in the sky and said to the only other human being beside me, “Do you see that blimp up there?”

“Huh?” he replied.

I pointed at it again. “That one! The one flying up there. Look, look!”

“Huh?” he repeated.

I raised my voice. “You don’t see things like that around here very often. This is the first time I’ve ever seen one. How about you?”

“Huh?”

By then, I was tempted to say, “Hey, buddy, look at the sky! Why do you keep staring at my finger?”

But I didn’t, because I already knew what his answer would be: “Huh?”

Being the last two of your species doesn’t mean you’re kindred spirits.

Why cling to the idea that someone has to understand you? Perhaps it’s better to simply point to the sky and say, “Hey, buddy, look at that blimp up there.”

Then let him look at the sky, the ground, the blimp, or even your finger. As long as you know what you’re looking at, that’s good enough.
