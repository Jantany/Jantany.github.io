I think, therefore, we are.

One body, one mind, one person, two complete sets of memories, two people, one body, one mind…

On Earth in 1887, Ansel Bourne, a clergyman from the town of Coventry in Rhode Island, a tiny state of the United States, went missing on the way to his bank to withdraw some money. Two weeks later, he appeared in Norristown in Pennsylvania, a neighboring state of that nation, calling himself Alfred J. Brown. He had rented a room there and opened a small storefront, selling stationery and candy. In that new life, he was not Bourne masquerading as Brown. He was entirely Brown and not Bourne at all. However, his time there as Brown was brief. Less than two months later, one morning Bourne awoke in Norristown with no recollection of his life as Brown, surprised to find himself living in a totally strange place, unable to understand or explain himself. Earth people referred to his condition as Dissociative Fugue, an extremely rare psychological disorder: one brain unable to live with two minds together. One skull, two minds, incompatibility, flight, fugue.

This is the Bourne-Brown story I heard from a renowned psychiatrist and religious leader. At that time, he also told me of other real-world terrestrial cases that revealed how the human brain on Earth responds when faced with multiple, conflicting sets of memories: doctors there called it Dissociative Identity Disorder, Schizophrenia, Acquired Savant Syndrome, and so on.

“The primary consciousness of the terrestrial human brain seems unable to control two simultaneous complete sets of life memories. It cannot fully run two distinct memory-based identities in parallel. But we,” he said pointing at both me and himself, “under the right circumstance or treatment, can.”

Yes, we can.

Because we are Uniques.
